# Opening chapter (draft): "Headroom"

> **Status: proposal, not canon.** A player-facing draft of the chapter that
> introduces New London and the people of *The Quiet Dose* **before** Elias
> Ward's death (brief §16 item 7). It assumes the recommended Option A of
> `the-quiet-dose/hidden-truth.md`, and every fact the later evidence relies on
> is planted here (see "Planted facts" at the end). Choices are written as
> prose sketches, not as game logic.
>
> Two decisions are deliberately left open and marked in the text:
>
> - **⟦H⟧ marks a Holmes-slot line**: wording that depends on whether Holmes
>   is alive, reimagined or represented by a successor (brief §15). The text
>   uses a version that works for all three; the table at the end gives the
>   variant for each.
> - **⟦P⟧ marks a player-identity line**: the player's background is open
>   (brief §15). The text uses a neutral version; options are at the end.

---

## 1. Baker Street

The rain in New London comes off the estuary warm. It has crossed forty
kilometres of heat-exchanger country to get here, and by the time it reaches
Marylebone it smells faintly of salt and hot metal, like a kitchen where
someone has been soldering.

You let yourself into the rooms at 221B with a key that is older than the
door. The landlady's house system greets you by name and tells you, as it
has every morning this week, that the building is on a priority contract
"by long-standing arrangement" and that no brownout has touched this address
in a hundred and nine years.

⟦P⟧ *Your licence is on the wall, still in its envelope: **Licensed Appeal
Investigator, Coroner's Appeal Bench of New London.** Three weeks old. You
have not yet been retained by anyone.*

⟦H⟧ *On the desk lies the casebook: a heavy, ordinary paper notebook, the
kind nobody makes any more. Its margins are full of notes in a small, hard
hand. The newest one, in ink still dark, is a single line:*

> *Data first. Theory after. The world will try to give you them the other
> way round.*

*(Doyle: "It is a capital mistake to theorise before one has data."
text:the-adventures-of-sherlock-holmes.txt#L173-L174)*

The door chime goes. On the step is a man of about forty in a docker's
weather shell, turning a cap in his hands.

## 2. The client with too much dose

His name is **Danny Pruitt**. He works the gig halls at Rotherhithe, hiring
by the shift onto whatever needs hands: cask handling, salt-loop flushing,
the dirty end of the Heatline. He is polite, and frightened, and trying not
to show either.

"They've stopped calling me," he says. "The hall's AI. Four months ago I had
headroom. Now my record says I've had nineteen millisieverts this year and
the good jobs want people under five. I haven't been near anything hot. I
swear I haven't. But the record's the record."

He shows you the record on his band. It is neat, official and certain: a
small dose every week, rising steadily since March.

> **Choice (sketch).** Ask what jobs he has done since March / ask where he
> keeps his dosimeter / ask what "headroom" means. All three are answered
> before he leaves; the order changes only his warmth toward you.

**If asked about headroom:** "It's what you've got left before the limit.
Contractors want people with plenty, so they can put them anywhere. A man
with headroom is worth twice a man without." He laughs, without humour.
"They sell it like a commodity. Fresh headroom, they say."

**If asked about the dosimeter:** "On my coat. Well. On the hook, at the
hall, when I'm not on shift. Everyone does. You're not allowed to take them
home."

He can't pay much. He can pay something. You take the case.

⟦H⟧ *When he has gone you open the casebook to write him up and find,
on the page before, an older note in the same hard hand:*

> *A witness tells you where it has been. Find out where that was.*

## 3. Rotherhithe

The gig hall is a converted warehouse on the river, loud with the
announcements of the hiring AI, which reads out shifts and pay rates in a
voice designed to sound like nobody in particular. A hand-painted sign over
a trestle table at the back says **LINE CREWS' UNION: FREE ADVICE ON YOUR
DOSE RECORD. SATURDAYS.**

Two people sit behind it.

The man is in his early sixties, broad and grey, in a cardigan with
a pen in every pocket. When he stands to shake your hand he does it
carefully, the way big men do who have learned to be gentle with small
equipment.

"Elias Ward," he says. "I'm an engineer at Erith Reach. On Saturdays I'm
whatever the union needs. This is my daughter, Mara, who is here under
protest."

"Not under protest," says the young woman beside him, not looking up from
three screens of scrolling logs. "Under contract. The city's auditing the
hiring AI. It's been marking people down for low headroom and I want to
know if it's doing it fairly." She looks up. "You're the investigator? Danny
said he'd found one."

**Elias Ward** reads Danny's record the way some people read music. "A small
dose every week," he says, "always on the same days. Tuesdays and Fridays.
Danny, what shifts do you work?"

"Mondays, Wednesdays. Sometimes Saturday."

"Then on Tuesdays and Fridays, where is your dosimeter?"

"On the hook."

Elias nods. "Let's go and look at the hook."

Before you go through the inner door, he stops you and holds out a small
white badge on a clip.

"Visitor dosimeter," he says. "The hook's beside a controlled lane."

"It's only a coat hook."

"Then it'll read nothing, and you'll have lost nothing by wearing it." He
clips his own to his cardigan pocket with the ease of forty years. "I've
never walked into a controlled area without one in my life. Not once. My
wife used to say I'd wear one to my own funeral."

Mara, behind him, rolls her eyes with the deep affection of an only child.

### 3.1 The hook

The coat hooks are on the wall of a corridor. The far side of that wall,
behind a yellow line and a door with a trefoil on it, is the **cask lane**,
where shielded transport casks from the Erith Reach decommissioning wait
for the river barge. The barge comes on Tuesdays and Fridays.

Elias shows you the lane's posted survey: dose rates at the boundary,
marked in microsieverts per hour. He lets you work it out.

> **Scientific Insight unlock: Radiation dosimetry I (dose rate × time).**
> *(Interactive sketch: a dose-rate field, a clock, and a dosimeter that can
> be dragged around. Dose accumulates as rate × time; move it away and the
> rate falls.)* Proposed numbers for review: SQ-06.

The answer is plain once you see it. A dosimeter hanging all night through
the wall from a waiting cask collects a small, steady dose. Twice a week,
for months. Danny's badge has been honestly recording the place where it
hung.

"It's not lying," Elias says. "That's the thing people get wrong about a
dosimeter. It's an honest witness to where *it* has been. It can't tell you
where *you've* been. Danny wasn't on that hook. His badge was."

> **Scientific Insight unlock: What a dosimeter records.** A personal
> dosimeter measures radiation arriving at the dosimeter. It says nothing
> about what happened to a body it was not on, and nothing about anything
> inside a body. (SQ-15.)

Mara says, without turning round from her screens, "And the hiring AI took
the record as the man. Which it would." She makes a note. "I'm going to
enjoy writing this up."

## 4. The inspector

The man who walks into the corridor wears the dark green of the
Radiological Safety Directorate and the expression of someone who has
already read the file.

"Elias," he says. "I might have known."

"Jules." Elias's face opens. "This is the investigator I told you about.
Julian Marsh, Principal Inspector for Legacy Sites, East. That lane carries
our casks, so it's his."

**Julian Marsh** is Elias's age, lean where Elias is broad, with a
precise, dry courtesy. He shakes your hand, and then Mara's, and holds hers
a moment longer. "Mara. You look tired. Are you eating?"

"Uncle Jules." She hugs him briefly, and says to you, low, as he turns to
the lane survey, "He signed all my school forms when Mum was ill. Dad
couldn't face the paperwork. Neither could I, I was eleven."

Julian listens to your account, checks the survey, checks the hook, checks
the barge schedule on his band. "Yes," he says. "That's it. Well done."
And then: "The hall has been storing dosimeters beside a controlled lane
for at least four months. That's a reportable breach. I'll file it today."

"Jules," says Elias quietly. "Danny didn't put the hooks there. If you
report it as written, his name's on the breach and he's unemployable till
it clears. Could you not give the hall a week to move them?"

"No."

"Jules."

"Elias." Julian is not unkind. He is immovable. "I report everything.
Everything. The day I stop is the day the job stops. Danny's record will
be corrected; I'll put that in the report too, in writing, and the hall
will pay the fine, not him. But it goes in today." He glances at you. "You'll
find that's the only way this works. The moment an inspector starts deciding
which breaches matter, there are no rules, only favours."

> **Choice (sketch).** Side with Elias (Elias Affinity ↑, Julian Respect ↓) /
> side with Julian (Julian Respect ↑, Danny Affinity ↓) / propose that the
> report name the hall's storage policy as the cause (Julian Respect ↑,
> Elias Affinity ↑; Julian agrees, a little surprised: "That's what I was
> going to write. But yes."). None of these changes the facts planted here.

The old friends walk out together, arguing in the comfortable way of people
who have had the same argument for forty years.

"Thursday?" Elias says at the door.

"Thursday. I'll be late. I've got orphan-source paperwork from every
bankrupt yard in Dagenham to chase, and half of it says things were returned
to suppliers who've never heard of them." Julian sighs. "One of these days
I'll find out the stuff never went anywhere at all."

"You'll find it," says Elias. To you: "We eat together the first Thursday
of every month. Have for thirty years. Jules cooks. I bring the wine. It's
the only arrangement that's kept us both alive."

## 5. Smithfield

To clear Danny formally you need an independent readout of his badge
history, and the union's advice desk sends you to the one place in the city
that will do it for a docker's fee: the **Voss Bioassay Laboratory**, three
rooms over a pharmacy in Smithfield, across the square from Barts.

A brass plate by the door: *Supported by the Vale Foundation.*

**Dr Evelyn Voss** is in her fifties, small and quick, with rolled sleeves
and reading glasses pushed up into grey hair. She takes Danny's badge,
checks its serial against the issuing record, logs it into a chain-of-custody
form before she will so much as look at it, and only then says hello.

"Sorry," she says. "Habit. A result is only worth the paper that says where
the sample has been."

> **Scientific Insight unlock: Chain of custody.** (SQ-25.)

While the reader runs, she shows you round, because she likes showing
people round. The rooms are full of instruments you have only seen in
pictures, every one carrying a calibration sticker with a date and a
reference number.

"Every one of these is traceable to the Metrology Institute's standards,"
she says, "which means that if I and another lab measure the same thing and
disagree, there's a referee." She taps a heavy shielded chair in the corner,
a whole-body counter. "And every one of them is blind to something. That
one listens for radiation that gets out of a body easily. It's very good at
it. It would sit quite happily next to a person full of something it isn't
built to hear, and tell you, truthfully, that it heard nothing."

> **Scientific Insight unlock: Every instrument is built to see some things.**
> What a whole-body counter can and cannot detect; why a negative result
> answers only the question the instrument asks. (SQ-18, SQ-19.)

"That sounds like a weakness."

"It's not a weakness. It's a specification. The weakness is in people who
forget what question they asked." She looks at the reader. "Your docker's
badge has been on a hook beside a cask lane. Tuesdays and Fridays. You knew
that."

The door opens. Elias Ward comes in with a folder under his arm, sees you,
and stops.

"Ah," he says.

"Mr Ward," says Dr Voss, very formally, a fraction too quickly. "You'll be
bringing the union's batch."

"The union's batch," he agrees. He puts the folder on her desk. It is
not the union's colour. "I'll not stop."

He doesn't. When he has gone, Dr Voss is very busy with the reader for a
while.

> **Observation (optional).** A player who notices may write in the journal:
> *Voss called him "Mr Ward". He called her nothing at all. The folder was
> not the union's.* No dialogue option exploits this now; it matters later.

## 6. Belvedere Flats

On the following Saturday, the Vale Foundation opens a clinic in Belvedere
Flats: free care for decommissioning workers and their families, with a
dosimetry advice desk modelled, the press release says, "on the Line Crews'
Union's volunteer service at Rotherhithe". Danny has been cleared and wants
you to come. So does Mara, who is auditing the clinic's public AI and
complains about it all the way down on the estuary line.

From the platform you can see **Erith Reach**: the old HTGR station, its
reactor buildings wrapped in scaffolding and its turbine hall half
dismantled. Beyond it, on the horizon, the clean white bulk of the Tilbury
LFTR park, where the city's power comes from now.

"Dad's worked there since he was eighteen," Mara says. "When it closes he'll
have signed off every last package of it. Some people here hate him for
that." She nods at the tower blocks of Belvedere. "Some people here hate
everyone."

The clinic is new, bright and genuinely good. The waiting room has a play
corner, a bank of free terminals on the priority tier, and a nurse who
remembers people's children's names. You can talk to a dozen residents here
(optional), and none of them has a bad word for the clinic.

**Anthony Vale** speaks for four minutes. He is slight and
plainly dressed, and he does not use notes.

"This estuary kept the city alive for eighty years," he says. "The people
who did that work were promised that the city would look after them in
return. It has not always kept that promise. This building is a small
payment on a large debt." He pauses. "It will not be the last."

A voice from the back: "Will it give us our jobs back?"

The man pushing forward is in his forties, in a union jacket, with a face
that has been angry for a long time. **Tobias Crane**, Mara murmurs, steward,
laid off from Erith Reach fuel handling two years ago.

Vale does not flinch. "No, Mr Crane. A clinic cannot. The phase-out is
right; the old stations are worn out, and the new ones are safer and
cleaner. But it was right for the city and hard on you, and nobody should
pretend those are the same thing."

Crane stares at him. He was ready for anything but agreement. "Pretty
words," he says, and goes, but slower than he came.

Near the door, a man in a Batemann & Calder site jacket, **Gideon Hale**,
radiation protection manager at Erith Reach (Mara tells you), is arguing
with the clinic's manager about whose dosimetry service the advice desk will
use. "Ours is free to them," he is saying. "Free and accurate." The manager
does not look convinced.

### 6.1 Vale

Afterwards, Vale finds you. He has heard about Danny.

"You looked at the hook instead of the man," he says. "Most people look at
the man. Most systems certainly do." He studies you with what seems to be
real interest. "If you ever need a laboratory you can trust, Dr Voss's is
the best in the city. I'm only her funder. I don't get to see her results
before anyone else does, and I'd stop funding her if I did."

> **Choice (sketch).** Thank him / ask why he funds her / ask what he wants.
> Every answer is true and none is complete. *(Ask what he wants:* "For the
> machines to keep running and the people who run them to be treated
> decently. I find the first is easier than the second.")

Then he sees Elias across the room, and his face changes: not much, only to
the attention of a man who has recognised a name.

"Mr Ward. Elias Ward. You wrote the Erith Reach graphite papers in the
sixties." He shakes Elias's hand warmly. "I've read them more than once. You were
at Erith Reach in '58, weren't you?"

Something passes over Elias's face, very quickly. "I was," he says. "Most of
us were, then."

"Then you're one of the people I meant in my speech," says Vale, and is
called away.

> **Note for the owner.** Vale's question is written to be innocent in
> Option A (he has read the papers) and to bear a darker reading if the owner
> later decides Vale knew of the 2158 event. Nothing here settles it.

## 7. The walk to the station

Elias walks you and Mara to the estuary line in the dark. Mara is on a call
with her office. The Heatline pipes keep the pavement warm; the rain steams
off it.

He is quiet for a long time, and then he says, "Can I ask you something
professional?"

"Of course."

"If someone wanted to appeal a finding. An old one. Thirty years old. Is
there a limit? A time after which it doesn't count any more?"

> **Choice (sketch).** Answer honestly that you do not know and will find
> out / say that a finding procured by a false record can be reopened at any
> time on fresh evidence (this is the proposal's legal rule; flagged for the
> owner) / ask whose finding. If asked whose: "Hypothetically," he says, and
> smiles, and the smile does not stay.

"Hypothetically," he says, "suppose the person who'd appeal it is also one
of the people who'd be in trouble if it were reopened."

"Then they'd need a lawyer."

"They'd need a friend," says Elias. "I have one of those. That's the
difficulty." He looks at Mara, ten steps ahead, laughing at something on
her call. "There's something I have to do before long. I've been putting it
off for thirty years. It's too serious to talk about yet." He shakes his
head. "Come to dinner one Thursday, when it's done. Jules cooks. You'll like
him; everyone does."

*(Cf. Violet Westbury on Cadogan West: "It is too serious for me to speak
about, even to you" (text:his-last-bow-03-the-bruce-partington-plans.txt#L644-L647).)*

The train comes. He waves you both on, and walks back along the warm
pavement towards Erith, a big grey man under the rain, with a dosimeter clipped
to his cardigan pocket though he is nowhere near anything at all.

⟦H⟧ *That night, writing it up, you find you have filled a page with Elias
Ward and half a line with Danny Pruitt. In the margin, in the small hard
hand, an old note you had not noticed before:*

> *The client is not always the case.*

## 8. Weeks

*(A short montage, playable as two or three optional small jobs that teach
the city: a tenancy dispute over a grid-tier downgrade in Aldgate; a lost
cask manifest at the Woolwich archive; a gig-hall worker's medical bill. The
player can meet Hale again, Crane again, and Voss twice; none of these jobs
touches the Wards. This is where the world exists beyond the main mystery,
brief §12.9.)*

On a Wednesday, three weeks and some days after Belvedere, your band shows
a message from Mara Ward:

> *Dad collapsed at work today. He's in Barts. They're saying radiation.
> He'd want you to know. I don't know why I'm telling you. M.*

Twelve days later:

> *He died this morning.*

Five days after that:

> *The coroner says it was the job. Occupational exposure. There's a
> payment if I don't appeal. Uncle Jules helped me with the forms.*
>
> *It wasn't the job. He never walked into anything without a dosimeter in
> his life. You know that. You were there.*
>
> *Will you take the case?*

**END OF THE OPENING CHAPTER.** *The Quiet Dose begins.*

## How the Holmes decision can be made later (⟦H⟧ variants)

The draft never shows the Consultant in person and never names them. All
three ⟦H⟧ lines are margin notes in a casebook. The owner can choose:

| ⟦H⟧ line | H1. Holmes alive (very old; speculative longevity medicine; retired, counsels by note) | H2. Holmes reimagined (a 2189 consulting detective in the canon's spirit, perhaps with a new name) | H3. Successor (the Consultant is gone; the player inherits the practice and its casebooks) |
|---|---|---|---|
| §1 "Data first…" note, ink still dark | As written: he wrote it this morning; he is upstairs, or abroad, and writes rather than speaks | As written: the player's mentor-partner left it; they will appear in person in a later chapter | Change "ink still dark" to "ink long faded": the note is decades old, and the player is reading the dead |
| §2 "A witness tells you where it has been" | A current note, perhaps left after reading the player's booking diary | Same | An old note the player happens upon; coincidence becomes inheritance |
| §7 "The client is not always the case" | A note that was not there that morning; he is watching | Same; the mentor is watching | "An old note you had not noticed before" (as written) |
| 221B's century-old priority contract "by long-standing arrangement" (§1) | He has paid it for a very long time | The practice has paid it | The estate pays it; the player is a beneficiary |

In every variant the player solves the case. The Consultant's notes give
method, never answers (brief §12.10). The notes paraphrase canon methods;
they do not quote modern adaptations.
